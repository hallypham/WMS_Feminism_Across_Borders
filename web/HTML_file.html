The Four Waves of Feminism

First Wave

Focused on legal rights and suffrage, largely centered on white Western women.

Second Wave

Expanded to workplace equality, reproductive rights, and social roles, but still often universalized Western experiences.

Third Wave

Emphasized diversity, intersectionality, and challenged the idea of a single feminist identity.

Fourth Wave

Driven by digital activism, global solidarity, and attention to structural violence across borders.

Content Reflection: Feminism Across Borders

Feminism Across Borders: Understanding Culture, Religion, and Global Power

Feminism has never been a single story. Although the four waves of feminism shaped major milestones in gender equality, they emerged primarily from Western contexts and often failed to represent the realities of women across cultures, religions, and political systems. As I explore transnational feminism and Lila Abu-Lughod’s Do Muslim Women Need Saving?, I see clearly how global feminist conversations must move beyond universal assumptions and toward deeper cultural understanding.

The four waves of feminism provide a historical foundation. The first wave fought for suffrage and legal rights, but centered mostly white Western women. The second wave expanded into workplace equality and reproductive rights, yet still often treated Western experiences as the norm. The third wave introduced intersectionality, acknowledging race, sexuality, and identity. The fourth wave, shaped by digital activism, pushes feminism into global spaces. However, even with this evolution, Western feminist narratives still dominate public discourse, shaping how people imagine “women’s issues” worldwide.

This is where transnational feminism becomes essential. The Everyday Feminism article argues that feminism must account for how colonialism, capitalism, religion, migration, and global politics shape women’s lives differently. Instead of assuming that Western feminist goals apply everywhere, transnational feminism asks us to listen to women within their own cultural contexts. It challenges the idea that liberation looks the same in every society. This perspective helps dismantle stereotypes about Muslim women, Asian women, African women, and others who are often portrayed as oppressed without understanding their lived realities.

Abu-Lughod’s critique of the “saving Muslim women” narrative highlights how Western governments and media use women’s suffering as political justification for military intervention. After 9/11, Afghan women’s oppression became a moral reason for war, even though the narrative ignored the complex social, historical, and political factors shaping their lives. Abu-Lughod argues that Muslim women do not need saving in the Western sense; they need justice defined within their own communities. Her work aligns with transnational feminism by rejecting simplified rescue fantasies and emphasizing context, agency, and respect.

Reflecting on these ideas, I recognize how my own cultural background shapes my understanding of feminism. Growing up outside the Western world, I saw gender norms influenced by tradition, family expectations, and religion. Some practices outsiders might label “oppressive” were, in context, expressions of identity, spirituality, or community belonging. Reading these texts helped me see how easily Western perspectives can misinterpret cultural practices. I now understand feminism as a global, collaborative movement that must be rooted in listening rather than imposing.

Ultimately, feminism across borders requires humility, curiosity, and a commitment to justice that respects cultural diversity. Women’s lives are shaped by more than gender alone—they are shaped by history, politics, religion, and global power. A truly inclusive feminism must embrace these complexities.
